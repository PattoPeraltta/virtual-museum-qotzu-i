{EXHIBIT-1} 
The Nación Originaria Uru
<b>The Ancestral Nation</b><br>
The Nación Originaria Uru is an officially recognized indigenous nation in the Republic of Bolivia, and its communities are native to the water-locked altiplano plateau that stretches across southern Peru, western Bolivia, and northeastern Chile.  Historically, the Urus lived on artificial islands of totora reeds in the lakes and rivers that make up the Titicaca/Desaguadero/Poopó/Salares basin (TDPS). In the modern day, the Urus are now settled in three primary zones: on the southern and western coasts of Lake Titicaca, and in the Bolivian municipality of Chipaya north of the Coipasa Salt Flat, and on the eastern banks of Lake Poopó and the Desaguadero River. 
<br><br>
<b>The Three Communities</b><br>
The communities of Lake Poopó, who used to identify as Uru-Moratos, now self-classify as the Tres Comunidades Uru del Lago Poopó (“the three Uru communities of Lake Poopó”), encompassing the villages of Puñaka Tinta  María, Vilañeque, and Llapallapani. These three locations are the only remaining subset of the Uru nation that had continued to rely predominantly  on the hydrological resources of Lake Poopó for subsistence into the twenty-first century.
<br><br>
<b>A Distinct Identity</b><br>
Crucially, Urus have always constituted a separate ethnic group than other indigenous peoples of Bolivia; they are neither Aymara nor Quechua. The earliest available documentation of the pre-Columbian empires even made this distinction, with the predecessors to the Inca placing “water-dwelling Uros” into distinct tribute categories and marginalizing them due to their subsistence on the lake. Spanish conquistadors subsequently continued this view of the Uru people, creating a dual-marginalization paradigm wherein the Europeans subjugated the indigenous world of the altiplano, and Urus were further disenfranchised by other indigenous groups within that world.

{EXHIBIT-2} 
Qotzuñi: People of the Lake

The word Qotzuñi comes from the Chholo, the now-extinct language once spoken by the Urus of Lake Poopó. Qot is the word meaning &quot;lake&quot; (bearing resemblance to &quot;qocha&quot; and &quot;quta&quot; in modern Quechua and Aymara), and zuñ means man, woman, or person. Together, <em>qotzuñi</em>, sometimes stylized <em>kot&apos;suñ</em>, means &quot;people of the lake.&quot; In times of abundant water, Uru-qotzuñi communities spent weeks at a time on floating islands of totora, subsisting off of the lake&apos;s water, fish, aquatic plants, and fowl.
<br><br>
Lake Poopó is at the center of <em>qot&apos;zuñi</em> culture. As other Andean cultures refer to <em>Pachamama</em>, or Mother Earth, Urus of Lake Poopó speak of <em>Qotamama</em>, or Mother Lake. Until the most recent severe drought, these Uru communities historically spent large periods of time living on boats and floating islands in the lake, fishing <em>pejerrey</em>, harvesting aquatic plants for consumption and domestic use, and hunting the 35 species of bird that inhabit the Titicaca/Desaguadero/Poopó/Salares basin. Their water-based culture made them the target of pre-Columbian discrimination, as they did not contribute to Inca and pre-Inca tribute or corvée labor demands. Due to their self-subsistence, they were seen as &quot;idle&quot; by the pre-colonial empires. They were sometimes forcefully removed from the lake and coerced to work on agricultural lands and assimilate. This process, sometimes called <em>aymarization</em> (the term for adopting Aymara language and culture), began in the pre-colonial period but intensified with the arrival of the Spanish.
<br><br>
The pressures of the colonial government, and later the independent state of Bolivia, eventually mounted for the communities to pay taxes and integrate economically. The result was the Uru transition &quot;<em>al seco</em>,&quot; or &quot;to dry land,&quot; around the beginning of the 20th century. The qotzuñis Urus made arrangements to live on previously Aymara-owned land, but did not secure any land for agriculture or grazing. In turn, they formed fishing cooperatives, selling catches in regional markets. After a mass pejerrey die-off near the turn of the century, reliance on fishing waned, and with the drying of the lake and the contamination of its waters, fishing is now no longer viable at all. While two of the three Uru communities of Lake Poopó practice small-scale agriculture, there is still food insecurity and severe economic hardship within all three communities.

{EXHIBIT-3} 
Mining
The Azanaques Mountains that border Lake Poopó to the east, part of the Andean Cordillera Central, have been mined for metals since pre-Columbian times. Known as the “Bolivian Tin Belt,” this region is home to large-scale mining operations that extract tin, silver, zinc, and <em>complejo</em>, or a mixture of various rare earth metals, from the mountains. Operations take three forms: state-owned (like Huanuni Tin Mine), private (like Inti Raymi Corporation) and cooperative (which are collectively-owned and unregulated). 
<br><br>
The refinement of these metals involves water-intensive processes that heavily divert and contaminate the streams that flow from the Andes into the endorheic basin terminating at Lake Poopó. The water that is not redirected for these processes is often subject to chemical leaching and overflow from tailing pools; dangerously high concentrations of arsenic, for instance, have been found in these streams and in Lake Poopó’s basin. Such chemicals have also increased the pH and salinity levels of the basin’s catchment, not only making it toxic to ingest, but also making evaporation more intense during dry months. In this sense, <b>contamination has both led to the decimation of Poopó’s water quality and a reduction in the basin’s levels.</b> Even groundwater, once thought immune to these trace chemicals, has been contaminated.
<br><br>
Sadly, with the loss of the lake’s aquatic resources, mining is one of the only economic opportunities left for the Urus of Lake Poopó. As a result, many members of the community have no choice but to work in the many cooperative mines that dot the Cordillera Central, where labor, safety, and contamination regulation is all but entirely absent.

{EXHIBIT-4} 
Lake Poopó
Lake Poopó, a partially saline body of water in the Department of Oruro, was once the largest lake entirely contained within the country of Bolivia. Covering up to 3,000 km<sup>2</sup>, it formed the southernmost basin of the Titicaca-Desaguadero-Poopó-Salares (TDPS) system, an enclosed hydrological network that has shaped the Andean landscape for millennia. The lake&apos;s shallow depth—averaging just 1.5 meters—made it highly sensitive to both seasonal precipitation and long-term climatic trends, but was deep enough to support several species of fish, including the introduced <em>pejerrey</em>, and 35 species of birds, including Andean flamingos. 
<br /><br />
Lake Poopó has always been prone to dramatic fluctuations in water level, and its tendency to evaporate during periods of drought was first written about in 1589 by Spanish colonizers. However, recent decades have seen an unprecedented decline. Strong El Niño events—such as in 1983, 1997-98, and 2014-16—have drastically reduced water levels, culminating in the complete desiccation of the lake in 2015. Rising temperatures and shifting precipitation patterns, exacerbated by climate change, have accelerated this drying trend. Meanwhile, increasing global demand for quinoa and tin has led to unregulated upstream water diversion and pollution, further degrading the basin&apos;s fragile ecosystem.
<br /><br />
<b>The Lifeblood of the Altiplano</b><br />
The Desaguadero River, which carries runoff from Lake Titicaca, has historically been the lake&apos;s primary water source. Additional inflows come from mountain streams like the Juchusuma, Pazña, and Huanuni Rivers, which originate in the mineral-rich Cordillera Central—an area heavily exploited for tin, silver, and other metals. However, these rivers are highly seasonal, flowing primarily between November and April. 
<br /><br />For over 10,000 years, the TDPS basin has sustained human life in the high Andes. The waters of Lake Titicaca and the Desaguadero River supported early agropastoralist communities and later served as a foundation for complex societies such as the Tiwanaku and Inca Empires. These civilizations engineered sophisticated irrigation systems to control water distribution, ensuring agricultural productivity in the arid altiplano. Today, as Lake Poopó faces an uncertain future, its story serves as a warning of the delicate balance between human activity and environmental sustainability.

{EXHIBIT-5} 
2016 Drought
In December 2015, the city of Oruro made a declaration that Lake Poopó had &quot;disappeared,&quot; triggering a global flood of popular media stories about the region and its hydrological circumstances. Due to the extremity of El Niño that winter, as well as warmer average temperatures and higher evaporation, the disappearance was attributed to climate change. While this was certainly true to an extent, there are more factors involved that led to the lake’s drying-up.
<br /><br />
<b>Diversion of water</b><br />
Mining of tin and other metals in the nearby Cordillera Central changed the chemical makeup of the water, raising its base temperature and making evaporation more likely. These operations also use water-intensive refinement processes, and divert large amounts of water from Lake Poopó&apos;s intake streams. 
<br /><br /><b>Agriculture</b>
Quinoa farming in the Titicaca and Desaguadero regions also divert large amounts of water from what would otherwise replenish Lake Poopó. Since the &quot;quinoa boom&quot; of the early the 2000s, demand for quinoa production increased drastically, and in turn so has the amount of water diverted to these upstream agriculturalists. These are generally not large-scale growers, but mid-size businesses and individual farmers.<br /><br /><b>The impact</b>
The extreme landscape of the altiplano provided a powerful and dramatic visual of fishing boats stranded in a desert that was once a thriving aquatic ecosystem; at the same time, the challenges currently faced by the three Uru communities of Lake Poopó, who depended on the lake for subsistence and, increasingly, the sale of fish in the local markets, provided an avenue for journalists and global environmental activists to tell an emotional story of both ecological and cultural loss.

{EXHIBIT-6} 
Artisanship and Adaptation
The Urus of Lake Poopó have practiced craftsmanship for as long as they have had their lacustrine way of life, as the weaving of <em>totora</em> reeds and other aquatic straw-like flora was necessary for the building of the rafts on which they lived and traveled. Generations of Urus have created artisan clothing, accessories, tools, structures, and spiritual items by hand. 
<br><br>
As a result, in times of serious drought (like the one faced for the past decade), Uru families have turned to selling their artisan goods to neighboring communities for sources of income. Female heads of household generally attend <em>ferias</em>, or street fairs, in nearby towns once a week, and occasionally visit larger cities like Oruro, La Paz, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz to sell their crafts. Since 2017, with a grant from the U.N. World Food Programme, the Uru women have formed an artisan cooperative to centralize craftsmaking.
<br><br>
The increasing importance of artisan sales—an effort led chiefly by women—has explicitly elevated the position of women more broadly in Uru society. In the authority meetings, women now have equal say to that of the men, and some women now hold official liaison positions to the Bolivian government.

{EXHIBIT-8} 
Language
The Uru nation, having established numerous pockets of communities in different parts of the TDPS hydrological axis over the span of centuries, has developed various distinct languages and dialects of the family Uchumataqu. The Urus of Lake Poopó (Uru-Muratos) historically spoke a now-extinct language called Chholo until the mid-20th century. Like other Andean languages, it lacked gender, and much of what is known about the language revolves around culturally-specific uses, like its words relating to water and the lake. (The word Qotzuñi comes from this language.) European linguistic anthropologists in the early 20th century attempted to codify this language, but the transition away from isolated lacustrine lifestyles, and the subsequent adoption of the Aymara language, caused the language to become lost.
<br /><br />
As Uru-Muratos transitioned to life on dry land in Aymara territory, they began to learn to speak the Aymara language in order to participate in the local economy. Modern Uru people often cite the discrimination and cultural oppression that accompanied this linguistic assimilation. Today, due to changing demographics in the Poopó region, many Urus grow up speaking Quechua along with Spanish and Aymara, and are trilingual.

<br /><br /><b>Revival</b><br />In the late 20th and early 21st century, an Uru authority named Daniel Mauricio Choque was determined to revive the traditional Uru language. Although Chholo had been entirely extinct, a related Uru language called Pukina was still continuing strongly in Chipaya, a large community of non-Qotzuñi Urus. Some scholars hypothesize that the relation between Chholo and Pukina is similar to that between Spanish and French, but others believe them to have been much closer.
<br /><br />
Choque, traveling frequently between Puñaca Tinta María and Chipaya, developed the educational cooperative that brings Uru language teachers from Chipaya to the Uru communities of Lake Poopó to teach Pukina to young students. To this day, students in the Uru communities of Lake Poopó continue to learn basic Pukina.
